00 Overview: the temple, the ten shots, and one frame

This document gives the whole shape of Golden Temple before the later documents take it apart. It names the place, the light, the ten shots, the nine world modules and the parts that run at startup. It then traces one frame at film time 24.5 seconds through every stage that touches it.

The thesis

Golden Temple is a film made from one deterministic world. The world is a Japanese temple complex at the golden hour, built from code at load time. It has no three-dimensional model file, no texture file, no high dynamic range image (HDRI) and no photograph. The film is 70 seconds long, 1920 by 1080 pixels, at 60 frames per second (FPS). Ten shots of seven seconds each show a close object first and then reveal the complex around it.

The design rests on three decisions. First, one site plan in metres is the only source of position data. Second, almost every surface is a three.js physical material with Graphics Library Shading Language (GLSL) code injected by the patch system. The sky, the light shafts, the dust motes and the incense smoke use their own shader materials. The water is a physical material with a large injected shader. Third, every frame is the weighted average of eight sub-frames that differ in camera offset, shutter time, sun direction and ambient-occlusion rotation. The average produces anti-aliasing, motion blur, soft sun shadows and clean ambient occlusion (AO) without a temporal denoiser.

Aha: The film is not stored. It is a pure function of the query string, the site plan and the shot table. A second run on the same code should reproduce the same pixels, because no module calls Math.random. The inputs that are not in the repository are the music track, the installed browser, the graphics processing unit (GPU) and its driver, and the ffmpeg binary. The source cannot prove that two GPUs or two driver versions give bit-identical results.

That claim has a limit. The exploration did not run the project. The claim describes the source logic, not a measured image.

The place: one plan in metres

The site plan lives in src/layout.js. It states the units and the axes in a comment. Positive X is east, positive Z is south, positive Y is up, and the courtyard ground is at Y equals zero. North is negative Z. The plan also draws an ASCII map of the complex. The map is a reading aid. The object below it is the data that the modules read.

The plan also holds the arched bridge, four processional paths, the gravel plaza and the viewing pavilion. It holds the enclosing corridor, the south gate, the bell tower and two side halls. The axes have visible consequences. A camera at positive Z looks north toward the hall. The sun comes from negative X, so it lights west faces. The long shadows fall east and slightly north. The hall faces south, so its front is in shade at this sun angle and its west side glows. The pagoda is west of the hall, so it catches the direct sun and stands in front of the bright sky from many cameras. The pond lies south of the hall and east of the pagoda. Its surface reflects both.

The light and the air

The sun is the second global input. src/core/env.js fixes its elevation at 6.8 degrees. The horizontal direction toward the sun is (-0.94, 0.34), normalised. That direction points west and slightly south. The sun colour is linear red, green and blue (RGB) 1.0, 0.70 and 0.43. A comment gives about 3300 kelvin. The direct sun intensity is 7.0. The sky shader uses a separate intensity of 20.0.

# src/core/env.js:10-18
// Sun: low golden-hour sun in the west-south-west.
export const SUN_ELEVATION = 6.8 * deg;
export const SUN_AZ_DIR = new THREE.Vector2(-0.94, 0.34).normalize(); // horizontal (x,z) direction TOWARD the sun

export const sunDir = new THREE.Vector3(
  SUN_AZ_DIR.x * Math.cos(SUN_ELEVATION),
  Math.sin(SUN_ELEVATION),
  SUN_AZ_DIR.y * Math.cos(SUN_ELEVATION)
).normalize();

The air is not three.js fog. The engine replaces the fog shader chunks with an exponential height fog. The haze layer has a 22 metre scale height and an extinction of 0.0013 per metre at ground level. A uniform air term adds 0.00022 per metre. The forward scattering uses a Henyey-Greenstein phase function with g equal to 0.78. A Henyey-Greenstein phase function sets how much scattered light continues toward the viewer. This combination gives bright warm air near the sun and cool grey air away from it.

The low sun also fixes the shadow length. At 6.8 degrees the tangent is about 0.119, so a shadow is about 8.4 times the height of the object that casts it. A 20 metre hall therefore casts a shadow about 168 metres long. The engine does not render that full shadow. It fits the shadow camera around a per-shot region and uses a bias and a normal bias to control acne. Shadows that fall outside the region are absent. The shot table therefore chooses the region with care. A wide crane shot uses a radius of 78 metres. The close shots use 21 to 28 metres, so the map spends its resolution on the close object. The one exception is shot 7, the gate macro. It uses a 40 metre radius to cover a subject at a 60 metre focus distance.

The fog is installed inside every material shader. The pond water shader calls the same fog function for its surface. The sky uses its own atmosphere model and disables the fog chunk, so the horizon blend is not the same code. The fog also reads up to 48 baked ambient volumes. A volume is a box that multiplies image-based lighting (IBL) by a dark tint. The hall interior and the space under each pagoda eave use these volumes to lose sky light without a light probe.

How a camera move is evaluated

A key holds a position, a look point and optional lens values. The evaluator does not interpolate the look point as a point. It converts each look point into a view direction and a look distance. With two keys the evaluator normalises a linear blend of the two unit directions. With three or more it builds a Catmull-Rom curve through the unit directions and normalises the result. The distance is interpolated in log space, and the look point is rebuilt as position plus direction times distance. This choice keeps a close-up to wide reveal smooth. A linear look-point interpolation would slow the pan near the close object and then whip it around at the end. The log distance keeps the perceived change of distance even.

With two keys the evaluator uses straight interpolation. With three or more keys it builds a Catmull-Rom curve through the positions and a second curve through the directions. The curve parameter is the eased progress, not the raw time. The reveal ease is a smoothstep raised to 1.35. It holds the close object for about the first third of the shot and then accelerates. The inout ease is a blend of a linear term and a smoothstep. The blend keeps some motion in the middle of the move.

Focus is special. If any key defines a focus distance, the evaluator interpolates the logarithm of the focus distance. A key that omits focus uses the shot-level focus. If the shot focus is look or is absent, the key uses its own look distance. If no key defines focus, the shot-level focus applies, or the look distance when the shot focus is look. The aperture and focal length interpolate linearly. The shot-level values fill gaps.

Two shots receive extra smoothing. Shot 4 uses a Gaussian sigma of 0.22 seconds. Shot 9 uses 0.45 seconds. evalShot (src/shots.js:159) applies optional temporal smoothing when a shot sets smooth. It calls evalShotRaw (src/shots.js:193) for each sample, and that function evaluates the keys at one local time. With smoothing on, evalShot averages 13 samples from minus 2.5 sigma to plus 2.5 sigma around the requested time, with Gaussian weights. Beyond the shot ends it reflects the raw path around the edge. It reflects position, direction and focal length, and it reflects the log look distance. This point-reflection keeps velocity continuous, so the average does not pull the camera toward a stop at the cut. The smoothing was added after tools/camcheck.mjs reported acceleration spikes.

Shot six is generated, not hand keyed. src/shotkeys/interior.js builds 29 keys from one continuous distance function. It starts 1.55 metres from the face and ends 12.1 metres away. The lens opens at about 61.6 millimetres and ends at 20 millimetres. The aperture opens from f/5.6 to f/8. Shot nine is also generated, with 29 keys, an arc-length path and a speed profile. Both use linear easing because the generated curve already carries the ease.

The film clock

A shot is evaluated at a local time from 0 to 7 seconds. A film time maps to a shot index and a local time. viewFromQuery does this for a still. The render loop does it for every frame. In both cases the film time is i * SHOT_DUR + tLocal.

setTime converts film time into shader time. It adds 37.0 seconds to the shader clock. The source gives no reason for that number. The likely effect is to move the wind, cloud and water noise to a different point in their cycles. With that offset, the first frame does not start at the phase origin. This reading is an inference, not a source comment. The function also moves the cloud layer and calls every module update callback.

# src/main.js:169-174
function setTime(T) {
  G.uTime.value = T + 37.0;
  pipeline.time = T;
  SKY_UNIFORMS.uCloudOffset.value.set(T * 0.004, T * 0.0016);
  for (const fn of updates) fn(T, ctx);
}

The cloud offset moves 0.004 units per second in X and 0.0016 units per second in Y. Over 70 seconds the layer drifts slowly. The modules receive the film time, not the shot-local time. A plant therefore keeps one continuous wind phase across a cut, while the camera jumps to a new shot.

The nine world modules

The entry module declares the module names in one array. The order matters. Terrain builds first, because the pond reads the terrain field. The props module builds eighth. It adds the suspension parts later, in placeAnchors, after every module has built. The vegetation module must follow the terrain module, because it calls ctx.modules.terrain.heightAt to place plants. It comes last because no later module needs it. Its last position is only the declared order in ALL_MODULES.

# src/main.js:32-32
export const ALL_MODULES = ['terrain', 'pond', 'hall', 'interior', 'pagoda', 'bridge', 'buildings', 'props', 'vegetation'];

Each module has one entry file in src/world/; some modules also use supporting files. The loader imports the entry file at run time. It calls the exported build(ctx) function and expects an object. The loader adds the returned object to the scene and collects the update callback. A failure in one module is logged and does not stop the other modules.

# src/main.js:87-104
async function buildWorld(names) {
  const results = {};
  for (const name of names) {
    const t0 = performance.now();
    status(`building ${name}...`);
    try {
      const mod = await import(`./world/${name}.js`);
      const res = normalizeResult(await mod.build(ctx));
      res.module = mod;
      if (res.object) { res.object.name = res.object.name || name; scene.add(res.object); }
      if (res.update) updates.push(res.update);
      results[name] = res;
      ctx.modules[name] = res;
      console.log(`[world] ${name} built in ${(performance.now() - t0).toFixed(0)} ms`);
    } catch (e) {
      console.error(`[world] module "${name}" FAILED:`, e && e.stack ? e.stack : e);
    }
  }

The startup parts

Startup is a fixed sequence. Each step prepares data for the next step.

  1. Read the query string. Select the mode, the size, the quality preset and the sub-frame count.
  2. Install the fog shader replacement before any material compiles.
  3. Create the renderer with anti-aliasing off, tone mapping off and shadow map type percentage-closer

filtering (PCF).

  1. Create the scene, the camera, the sky dome and the sun light.
  2. Create the post pipeline with four-sample multi-sample anti-aliasing (MSAA), 10 or 16 AO

samples and the preview or final depth-of-field setting.

  1. Build the world modules in order and collect their objects, updates, anchors and volumes.
  2. Add the hall interior volume and the module ambient volumes, then bake them into the fog shader.
  3. Compile every shader program in parallel with renderer.compileAsync.
  4. Capture the sky into a prefiltered mipmapped radiance environment map (PMREM). At final quality,

also capture the built scene and replace the sky-only environment.

  1. Run each module finalize step. The hall captures an interior environment here. The pond

removes the fallback ground where water should show.

  1. Enter still, render or development mode.

Step eight is a performance step. The first draw of a material compiles its shader. Without a pre-compile, the first frame would stall many times. renderer.compileAsync compiles every program in parallel where the driver supports parallel compilation. The call uses a temporary camera at (10, 4, 30) that looks north toward the hall with a 24 millimetre lens. That pose covers the courtyard and the main buildings from the south. The shader cache key from the patch system keeps the program count small, because materials with identical hooks share one program. The query string can set nopc to skip this step.

Step nine uses a cube camera and a PMREM generator. The cube camera renders six 90 degree views from the chosen point. The generator prefilters the cube into a mip chain with roughness levels. The result is a texture that a physical material samples for diffuse and specular ambient light. The sky capture point is (0, 2, 0). The scene capture point is (10, 4, 4), near the hall and the pond. Both cube captures use a size of 256 pixels.

The two environment captures are different. The first capture sees only the sky. It gives every material a warm sky reflection and a blue fill from the opposite side. The second capture sees the built scene. It adds the bounce from the gravel court, the vermilion walls and the pond. The second capture is skipped in preview quality, when fewer than four modules are loaded, or when the query string sets noenvcap.

One frame at 24.5 seconds

Film time 24.5 seconds is inside shot 3, the wind-bell shot. The shot index is 3 because floor(24.5 / 7) equals 3. The local time is 3.5 seconds. The shot is owned by src/shotkeys/pagoda.js. It has four keys and no smoothing value, so evalShot calls the raw evaluator.

The stages below follow the actual calls in src/main.js and src/core/post.js.

  1. evalShot(3, 3.5) applies the reveal ease. The progress fraction is 0.5. The eased value is

0.5 raised to 1.35, about 0.392. The evaluator interpolates position and view direction with a Catmull-Rom curve. It interpolates the log of the look distance and the log of the focus distance. It interpolates the focal length and the aperture linearly. The result is one camera description. It has a focal length of about 28.6 millimetres at this time; the shot spans 40 to 16.5 millimetres. It also has a focus distance, an aperture, a near plane of 0.04 metres and a shadow region.

  1. postFor merges the default post settings with the shot post settings. Shot 3 turns depth of

field (DOF) on. The focus distance and the aperture come from the camera keys.

  1. The frame sets G.uShot to 3. The pagoda backlight shader reads this value. It switches the

rim-light rig that suits the shot.

  1. The frame sets the fog density. Shot 3 has no fog override, so the multiplier is 1.0.
  2. setShadowExclusions receives null, because shot 3 has no noShadow list. All shadow casters

stay enabled.

  1. pipeline.beginFrame() clears the accumulation target to black. The sub-frame counter returns

to zero.

  1. The render loop computes eight Gaussian weights for eight sub-frames. It then runs eight

sub-frames.

  1. For each sub-frame k, the loop computes a shutter time from a permuted index. It calls

setTime(24.5 + off). This updates the shared time uniform, the cloud offset and every module animation.

  1. The loop evaluates the camera again at 3.5 + off. It applies the position, the look target,

the lens and a projection-matrix offset from the Halton sequence. A Halton sequence is a list of points that fills a square evenly without a regular grid. The offset places the sample inside a two-pixel footprint.

  1. The loop fits the sun shadow camera around the shot region. The region centre is (-34, 9, -5)

and the radius is 21 metres. It adds a small jitter in the solar disc. The jitter is in radians, and its outer value is slightly larger than the 0.27 degree solar radius. The jitter follows a golden-angle spiral that depends only on k.

  1. pipeline.subframe renders the scene into a half-float red, green, blue, alpha (RGBA) target

with four-sample MSAA and a floating-point depth texture. It computes screen-space ambient occlusion (SSAO) at half resolution. It rotates the AO sample pattern by the sub-frame counter. It blurs the AO with a depth-aware two-pass filter. Volumetric light is off for this shot. It adds the result into the accumulation target with the Gaussian weight.

  1. After eight sub-frames, pipeline.endFrame runs the frame-level passes. God rays are off

because the shot does not set them. DOF runs, because the shot sets it. Bloom runs over seven mip levels. The final pass applies the tone map, the white balance, the lift, gamma and gain, the saturation, the contrast, the vignette, the chromatic aberration, the grain and the dither.

  1. In render mode the result goes to an unsigned-byte output target. The page reads it back with

readRenderTargetPixelsAsync and posts the raw bytes to the local render server. In still mode the result goes to the canvas.

The sub-frame time matters for animation. setTime runs inside the sub-frame loop, so wind, water ripples, smoke and cloud drift advance by a fraction of a frame for each sample. The camera also moves by the shutter offset. A moving leaf therefore blurs along its true path. The module update receives the unclamped film time, while the render-mode camera is clamped inside the current shot. This keeps animation continuous across a cut and keeps the camera inside one shot.

The key call is renderFrame. It owns the loop over sub-frames.

  P.exposure *= fade;
  pipeline.beginFrame();

Terms used in this document

  • Site plan: the L object in src/layout.js. It holds positions, sizes and routes in metres.
  • World module: one of nine build(ctx) units in src/world/. Each returns an object and optional

update and finalize functions.

  • Shot: one seven-second camera move. The film has ten.
  • Override: a module-owned replacement for a base shot entry, in src/shotkeys/.
  • Sub-frame: one sample of a frame. The render mode uses eight. A frame is their weighted average.
  • Accumulation: the additive buffer that receives every weighted sub-frame.
  • Ambient volume: a box that darkens IBL inside a building or under a roof.
  • PMREM: prefiltered mipmapped radiance environment map. It is the image-based light source.
  • Anchor: a position declared by a building for a hanging lantern or a bell. The props module

consumes anchors.

  • Showcase: a list of preview camera positions that a module returns for development.
